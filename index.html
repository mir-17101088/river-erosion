River Erosion in Bangladesh

Rivers have erased land six times the size of Dhaka in five decades

As riverbanks shift, families are displaced again and again. Many abandon rural life altogether and head for the capital's slums, only to end up in urban poverty.

Reporting:Pinaki Roy

The rivers, and the ground they take

Three great rivers braid down the middle of Bangladesh. Over fifty years they have swallowed land on a scale that is hard to picture. Here is the measure of it.

158,000 hectares

Over the past five decades, three rivers — the Jamuna, the Ganges and their confluence, the Padma — have eroded a combined 158,000 hectares of land, or about 1,585 square kilometres.

915 hectares more, this year

CEGIS estimates a further 915 hectares will erode this year, at the eleven reaches now marked along the rivers.

5× the capital

The land lost to erosion is nearly five times the area of two Dhaka City Corporations. The two rings, drawn to the map's own scale, are the measure of it.

4× even after the chars return

Newly formed chars, the sandbars that rise and fall with the rivers' shifting courses, have added back roughly 433 square kilometres (the inner ring). But the net loss, about 1,153 square kilometres, is still close to four times the capital — and it has displaced millions.

60% of land lost was engulfed by the Jamuna alone

Across the full 1973-2025 period, CEGIS satellite analysis shows the Jamuna alone accounted for three-fifths of everything lost in fifty years, more than the Ganges and Padma combined.

Researchers who study erosion-driven displacement describe a consistent sequence: families lose land, are displaced multiple times as chars shift beneath them, and eventually abandon rural resettlement altogether for the city.

Arriving with little formal education or skills, most are absorbed into the lowest-paid tiers of Dhaka's informal economy as day labourers, domestic workers or rickshaw-pullers and settle in the same handful of low-lying, flood-prone slums that already house earlier waves of erosion migrants.

From riverbank to slum

The land does not simply vanish. It moves people. And it moves them along a route that runs, again and again, to the same few corners of the capital.

Bholar Basti, a Dhaka slum now home to more than 30,000 people, mostly displaced by river erosion and flooding in Bhola district, is one such settlement. Migration researchers have documented similar clustering in Mirpur-12, Molla Bosti and Duaripara, all within the same corridor of northwest Dhaka. The pattern is a direct route from riverbank to slum, concentrating poverty in a few areas rather than spreading it across the country.

Ibrahim Mia is one such migrant. He once farmed more than three acres of land on the bank of the Jamuna in Chargilabari village, Islampur, in the northern district of Jamalpur, growing sugarcane, peanuts and rice.

"It was a happy life with my three children," he said, sitting at a tea stall in Dhaka's Karwan Bazar on a Tuesday afternoon.

When the river took his land and his house, Mia moved his family to his maternal uncle's home in Gaibandha. There, the only work available was as a day labourer in the fields, something he found difficult to accept. "It was a matter of dignity for me," he said.

In 2009, he left his wife, Sundari, and their three children behind and travelled to Karwan Bazar with a fellow villager. He has worked as a labourer in the capital's wholesale vegetable market ever since, earning about Tk 1,000 a day, of which roughly Tk 150 goes on food, bathing and a place to sleep.

I am working here and waiting for my land to emerge in the Jamuna. Once my land emerges through accretion, I'll go back to Jamalpur.

Ibrahim Mia, formerly of Chargilabari, Jamalpur

Bangladesh has no comprehensive count of people displaced by river erosion. Sudipta Kumar Hore, senior specialist for river, delta and coastal morphology at CEGIS, said the agency estimates roughly 1,000 people are displaced for every 100 hectares -- or one sqkm -- eroded.

A recent report by Change Initiative, a climate research organisation, projected that by 2050 one in every seven people in Bangladesh will have been displaced by climate change, with erosion accounting for about 26.6 percent of total displacement.

Government figures lag badly. The Department of Disaster Management's most recent erosion data date to 2019, when 24,328 families were recorded as victims of river erosion across nine districts — Dinajpur, Jamalpur, Munshiganj, Sherpur, Kurigram, Gaibandha, Chandpur, Mymensingh and Shariatpur — with damage estimated at Tk 366 crore. Even that tally did not cover all rivers and districts.

Independent researchers put the annual toll higher. Mohammad Zaman and Mostafa Alam, in their 2020 book Living on the Edge: Char Dwellers of Bangladesh (Springer), estimate that about 100 sqkm is lost to erosion every year; others cite a figure closer to 10,000 hectares annually.

For this year, CEGIS forecasts a further 915 hectares of erosion, putting about 73 established sites at risk across 11 locations in Gaibandha, Jamalpur, Kurigram, Pabna, Kushtia and Faridpur districts, including, by its estimate, four schools, three mosques, a health clinic and several roads. The agency excludes any predicted erosion area smaller than 100 metres from its forecasts, so the map is a floor, not a ceiling.

The Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB) acts on these predictions, primarily by deploying sand-filled geobags along vulnerable banks. Even so, tens of thousands of people lose land or property to erosion each year.

    Jamuna Lower Brahmaputra

    The embankment is the only thing holding the line.

    Along the Jamuna's right bank, the Brahmaputra flood embankment is doing double duty. Built to hold back floodwater, it's also the main defence against the river eating the bank itself. In 2025, even with that embankment in place, the Jamuna still took 348 hectares of land.

    CEGIS's 2026 forecast marks seven stretches as vulnerable along both banks, three on the right, four on the left, across Kurigram, Jamalpur, Gaibandha and Sirajganj.

    Across all seven, roughly 460 hectares of land and 64 hectares of settlement are at risk. Gaibandha faces the worst of it, at 223 hectares of land alone. Kurigram is projected to lose the most settlement, about 31 hectares, on top of 106 hectares of land. Sirajganj follows at 83 hectares.

    The human cost is concrete: two government primary schools in Gaibandha, three mosques, and Jamalpur's only community clinic are all expected to go under.

    Ganges

    A quieter forecast for the river that took the most in 2025.

    Of the three rivers, the Ganges actually did the most damage in 2025, about 593 hectares, well ahead of the Jamuna's 348 and the Padma's 93. The erosion front has been shifting south and west.

    But its 2026 forecast is comparatively narrow: just three vulnerable stretches, two on the left bank and one on the right, all in Pabna and Kushtia. About 320 hectares of land and 5 hectares of settlement are at risk.

    Pabna carries most of it, roughly 215 hectares, with no settlement flagged. Kushtia's share is smaller on land, about 105 hectares, but it's the only district where settlement and a school, one high school, are on the list.

    Padma

    One stretch, but a bigger bite than last year.

    The Padma took the least ground of the three rivers in 2025, about 93 hectares. CEGIS's 2026 forecast names just one vulnerable stretch on the Padma: a single site on the right bank, at Sadarpur in Faridpur.

    About 135 hectares of land and 4 hectares of settlement there are expected to erode this year — more land than the entire river lost in all of 2025.

    The long view

    CEGIS has run annual erosion forecasts for the Jamuna, Padma and Ganges since 2004, using satellite imagery gathered ahead of each monsoon season. On average, the agency's analysis shows, the three rivers have eroded about 30 sq km of land every year over the past five decades.

    But the trend within that average has been downward. Jamuna erosion averaged about 5,000 hectares a year in the 1980s; in recent years that has fallen to around 1,500 hectares. Padma erosion, which ran at roughly 2,300 hectares a year in the 1990s, has dropped below 1,000 hectares annually over the past decade.

    CEGIS data show the three rivers together eroded about 9,500 hectares in 1995 — 4,700 from the Jamuna, 3,600 from the Padma and 1,200 from the Ganges. By 2025, the combined total had fallen to 1,034 hectares, though the balance between rivers had shifted: 593 hectares eroded along the Ganges, 348 along the Jamuna and 93 along the Padma.

    A village that has moved three times

    In Kathuria village, in Shariatpur's Zajira upazila, the gap between prediction and protection was visible on a recent visit, when between 100 and 150 families were found still living directly on the Padma's bank. The river had already eroded the road behind the home of Mansura Begum and her husband, Mazid Gharami; ten days earlier, roughly half of their yard had gone into the river too.

    The couple have since built a makeshift shelter from corrugated tin salvaged from their old house. "The erosion started during the daylight. Villagers came to help us to dismantle the house and move our household things away," Mansura said. She recalled that the Padma had been about half a kilometre from their house a few years ago; last year it reached the property, and this year it took half the yard.

    Around forty BWDB workers were seen dropping sand-filled geobags into the river along the Kathuria bank during the visit, an effort that has so far protected what remains of the couple's yard. Their neighbours in nearby Majherkandi village were not as fortunate: the entire settlement was eroded away and its residents relocated to government land.

    At a tea stall on the Padma's bank, Wahab Khan, an elderly resident of Majherkandi, described a village that has now shifted location three times in his lifetime.

    1. ~40 years ago

      The original Majherkandi, on the opposite bank, was eroded away, scattering its residents.

    2. ~7 years ago

      A char had risen mid-river; villagers resettled and named it Majherkandi again. That char, too, was eroded seven years ago, forcing another move -- this time onto land belonging to the Bangladesh Bridge Authority (BBA), which periodically sends notices asking residents to vacate.

    3. 2007–08 onward

      When a new char formed around 2007-08, families relocated together and spent the next 15 years rebuilding: farmland, orchards of mango, jackfruit, blackberry, litchi, coconut and malta trees, and homesteads on what had once been 37 bighas of ancestral land.

    Fishing is now the village's main livelihood. "All the people have taken loans from NGOs, and we try to earn something just to repay the loan instalments," Wahab said. Abdur Rashid Bepari, another Majherkandi resident, said the family that once harvested 200 maunds of rice from its own land now depends on his two sons' wages as day labourers.

    Like Ibrahim Mia in Karwan Bazar, the villagers of Majherkandi are waiting for the river to give back what it took.
